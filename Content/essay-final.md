# Generation is cheap. Verification is scarce.

*ECON1626 Assessment 3, RMIT University. Tom Genovese, October 2026.*

AI is helping Australian finance produce far more work. Very little more of it is getting finished. Scroll to see why, and what to do about it.

---

## F1 The productivity puzzle

### The forecasts say boom. The numbers say flat.

> **Interactive: Guess first.** When firms gave coders AI agents, they wrote 30% more code. How much more finished work did they ship?
>
> *If the guess is above 12%:* You guessed +X%. The evidence says firms shipped at most 12% more, and the central estimate is nearer 6%. Most forecasts make the same assumption you did: that AI's extra work becomes extra output. Mostly, it doesn't.
>
> *If the guess is 12% or below:* You guessed +X%. That's inside what the evidence allows: at most 12% more, with a central estimate nearer 6%. You've already spotted the bottleneck that headline forecasts miss.

Microsoft and the Tech Council of Australia say generative AI could add up to **$115 billion a year** to the economy by 2030 (Microsoft & Tech Council of Australia 2023). The Productivity Commission expects productivity gains worth about **$116 billion** over a decade (Productivity Commission 2025).

Yet in the year to June 2026, Australia's GDP per hour worked **fell 0.2%** (ABS 2026b). AI is everywhere except in the productivity statistics.

The gap is not just a matter of time. It is a bottleneck. AI makes producing work cheap, but not checking it. When one link in a chain of tasks speeds up, the slowest remaining link sets the pace (Kremer 1993; Jones & Tonetti 2026). In knowledge work, that link is human verification.

**My forecast:** by 2030, AI will lift the volume of work in Australian financial services far faster than finished, compliant output, unless checking capacity is deliberately built. Finance is where this bites hardest, because an undetected error costs more there than almost anywhere else.

I use 2030 because it gives AI agents time to spread while regulation and review practices are still catching up.

**What AI can do by 2030.** The length of tasks AI agents can complete has doubled about every seven months for six years (Kwa et al. 2025). But reliability falls on longer tasks, and in one trial experienced developers were 19% slower with AI while believing they were 20% faster (Becker et al. 2025).

So my assumption is that AI's drafting ability keeps soaring, while its reliability and legal accountability do not. That gap is what someone has to check.

## F2 What happened in software

### Coders wrote 30% more. Firms shipped at most 12% more.

Software is where AI agents arrived first, so it previews other knowledge work. Chen and Stratton (2026) tracked about 726,000 workers in 718 firms before and after they adopted AI coding agents. Lines of code rose **30%**, commits 20% and pull requests 23%.

Finished work barely moved: the estimates rule out a rise larger than **12%**. At most, around 0.4 of the coding gain reached the final product. That share is the pass-through.

The rest piled up in review. Time to approve code rose **49%**, the share sent back for changes nearly doubled from 13% to about 25%, and comments per submission rose 35%. Firms moved people into checking, up 14%, while overall employment did not change.

AI review tools have not solved it: nearly 80% of the firms used them, yet AI wrote only 23% of review comments (Chen & Stratton 2026). AI raises the volume of drafts and can lower their quality; more reviewers fix only the first.

The result is "AI slop": unchecked output piling up, and no more finished products leaving the building.

> **Interactive: Approve the drafts.** You are the senior reviewer. Approve the AI's good drafts and reject those with an error. How many can you clear in 60 seconds?
>
> *End screen:* You checked N drafts. M were still waiting. You approved Y with an error and caught Z. The AI never slowed down; you were the bottleneck. That is the review queue Chen and Stratton measured, and finance drafts take longer to check.

## F3 Why finance bottlenecks harder

### Finance is next in the queue, and its checks are stricter.

AI use among Australian financial and insurance businesses jumped from **1% to 24%** between 2021–22 and 2024–25, the fastest rise of any industry and double the all-business average (ABS 2026a). The Commonwealth Bank, first in Asia-Pacific for AI maturity, is training its engineers in AI coding (Australian Broker 2026).

AI agents now draft credit assessments, statements of advice and compliance reports, and senior, accountable staff must check each against duties such as responsible lending. Even in an AI developer's own data, most use is augmentation, with people working alongside AI rather than handing tasks over (Anthropic 2025). Finished output can grow only as fast as checking capacity. **▶ Try it:** raise the AI boost and watch the queue.

Finance goes further than software. In Chen and Stratton's model, checking per draft rises with the cost of an undetected error, and in finance that cost is huge: six major institutions paid or offered **$4.7 billion** in remediation for fees charged for no service and non-compliant advice (Australian Broker 2023). Stricter checks make the bottleneck bigger here.

The regulator sees the gap: of 14 licensees planning to expand AI, **only one** upgraded its governance first (ASIC 2024).

**▶ Now try letting AI do the checking.** Push it past 60%.

> **Interactive: The checkout queue.** Each square is a draft; red ones contain an error. Humans catch 9 in 10 errors. AI checkers catch about 6 in 10 when lightly used, and fewer as they take more of the load, because AI checking AI shares its blind spots. Sliders: AI drafting boost, human checkers, checking per draft, AI checking share. If too many errors slip through, an alert reads: ⚠ ASIC ENFORCEMENT ACTION: errors are reaching customers.

## F4 Three scenarios to 2030

### Drafts rise 10% in every future. What differs is how much gets checked.

The model starts from today's sector: 552,900 workers (Jobs and Skills Australia 2025) and AI use at 24% of firms (ABS 2026a), rising to 72–80% by 2030. AI can draft or assist with about 44% of work hours (Microsoft & Tech Council of Australia 2023) and lifts output on that work by 30% (Chen & Stratton 2026). The baseline is today's trends continuing; the upside is what happens if checking goes right; the downside, if it goes wrong.

**Baseline: the review backlog.** Today's trends continue. Agents spread across most large firms by 2028, but senior reviewers stay scarce and each finance draft needs more checking than code. Drafts rise 9.5%; finished output just 1.1%. Pass-through is 0.12, against 0.20 in software. *Trigger:* None: this is the default path. *Watch:* Approval times and assurance hiring.

**Upside: verification catches up.** Checking goes right. Firms invest in checking and auditable AI handles routine checks, so almost every extra draft gets through: finished output rises 10.6%, about 8% after error costs. *Trigger:* ASIC and APRA guidance under existing powers. *Watch:* AI error rates falling.

**Downside: slop accumulation.** Checking goes wrong. Firms let AI rubber-stamp AI, so output looks strong at first. Then errors surface as breaches and remediation, and junior cuts shrink future checking capacity. By 2029 net output is 3.5% below a world without AI. *Trigger:* An AI-caused incident revives the paused guardrails. *Watch:* 72% of financial institutions use AI agents in underwriting, but only 3% say their data is fully ready (Australian Broker 2026).

**Testing the Industry Week claim.** At Industry Week, Dave Lemphers of Maincode argued that AI will change work profoundly, but that the opportunities it creates will outweigh what it displaces (Lemphers 2026); elsewhere he calls AI "a productivity lift for Australian businesses" (Lemphers 2025). The evidence supports him on jobs: adopting firms saw no fall in employment, and the IMF finds about half of exposed jobs in advanced economies are ones AI complements (Cazzaniga et al. 2024). It qualifies him on productivity: a lift in tasks is not a lift in output. His optimism holds only in the upside, so investment in checking is the hinge between his forecast and the baseline.

## F5 Policy playbook

### Don't write an AI law. Make sure someone checks the AI's work.

Australia has chosen not to pass a dedicated AI law: the National AI Plan put mandatory guardrails "on hold" (Department of Industry, Science and Resources 2025), and the Productivity Commission calls AI-specific rules a last resort, pending gap analyses (Productivity Commission 2025).

I accept that, but in finance the gap is already visible. ASIC's one-in-14 finding (ASIC 2024) is the gap analysis. The goal is to move the sector from baseline to upside with tools that exist, starting with APRA's CPS 230, which already requires firms to manage critical operations and report incidents (Brightlaw 2025).

**Can AI check the AI?** Partly. AI checkers add capacity cheaply, but share the drafting AI's blind spots, and a licensee, not a model, carries legal responsibility. Accountable, AI checking helps; as a rubber stamp, it produces the downside.

**Extending the Industry Week claim.** Lemphers frames "opaque black-box systems" as a sovereignty problem (Lemphers 2025). This forecast shows it is also a checking problem: the average transparency score of leading AI developers fell from 58 to 40 in a year (Stanford HAI 2026). Open-weight models, which firms can inspect, now trail the best closed models by only about two points (Stanford HAI 2025), so auditability need not cost capability.

> **Interactive: Policy dial.** Three policy credits, starting from the baseline:
>
> - **Guidance on checking AI output.** ASIC and APRA set expectations for checking AI output under existing powers. Trade-off: if it demands human sign-off on everything, it deepens the bottleneck. Measure: AI-related breaches and approval times.
>
> - **Build checking capacity.** Firms retrain staff as checkers, backed by training incentives. Fixes the volume problem. Trade-off: costs land before gains. Measure: review times.
>
> - **Protect the junior pipeline.** Keep graduate roles as apprentice checkers, so today's juniors become 2030's senior reviewers. Trade-off: a short-run cost while juniors look replaceable. Measure: graduate intake.
>
> - **Fund shared verification tools.** Shared checking tools mean each AI draft needs less checking: the only lever aimed at the quality problem. Trade-off: public money for private benefit. Measure: error and rework rates.
>
> - **Require auditable models.** Prefer open-weight or contractually auditable models, so firms can inspect what checks their work. 30% of the AI use cases ASIC reviewed relied on third-party models (ASIC 2024). Trade-off: less choice of vendor. Measure: share of models open to audit.
>
> - **Measure finished output, not drafts.** Free. Changes no outcome, but stops firms and governments misreading volume as productivity. Watch what the readout reports.

> **Interactive: You're the regulator.** Five decisions, one per year:
>
> - **2026:** A major bank wants AI to draft every home-loan assessment. *Allow, with human sign-off* or *Allow, sign-off optional*?
>
> - **2027:** Banks propose cutting graduate intake, since AI does junior work. *Fund apprentice checker roles* or *Let the market decide*?
>
> - **2028:** ASIC could issue guidance on checking AI output. *Issue guidance now* or *Wait for an incident*?
>
> - **2029:** Closed AI models are cheaper; open-weight ones can be audited. *Require auditable models* or *Let firms choose*?
>
> - **2030:** Treasury asks how to judge AI's success. *Count finished output* or *Count drafts produced*?
>
> *Outcomes:* You created the downside: unchecked AI, then a reckoning. You created the upside: checking kept pace with AI. You created the baseline: a growing review backlog.

## F6 Methods, data and uncertainty

### Every number here comes from a model you can change.

Each year the model compares the checking that AI-boosted drafts need with the checking people and AI can do. What cannot be checked becomes backlog, so pass-through is calculated, not assumed. With full adoption it reproduces the software results: drafts +30%, checking staff +14%, finished output +6% (Chen & Stratton 2026). Code, data and every assumption are in the project repository.

**Limitations.**

- **Transferability.** The core evidence is from software firms, mostly outside Australia. Applying it to finance is an assumption, not a measurement.
- **Timing.** The estimates cover about a year after adoption. Firms may reorganise over time, as with earlier technologies (Brynjolfsson, Rock & Syverson 2018), pushing outcomes toward the upside.
- **Structural breaks.** A leap in AI reliability would shrink the quality problem; a major AI-caused incident could trigger sudden regulation.
- **Measurement.** Much of finance output is imputed in the national accounts (Gordon et al. 2016), so AI's effects will be hard to see. Self-reported gains mislead too: 30% of users in a CSIRO trial reported no benefit (Whittle 2025).

**Use of AI.** I used Claude (Anthropic) to help find and summarise sources, structure the argument, draft text that I then edited, and write the code for the charts and interactives. I checked every figure against its original source.
