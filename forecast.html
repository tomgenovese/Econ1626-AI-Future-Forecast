> forecast --sector financial-services --region AU --to 2030

Generation is cheap. Verification is scarce.

AI is making Australian finance produce far more work. Very little of it is getting finished. This is a forecast of why, and what to do about it.

ECON1626 Assessment 3, RMIT University. Tom Genovese, October 2026.

F1The productivity puzzle

In 2023, Microsoft and the Tech Council of Australia estimated that generative AI could add up to $115 billion a year to the Australian economy by 2030 (Microsoft & Tech Council of Australia 2023). The Productivity Commission's final report on data and digital technology expects multifactor productivity gains above 2.3% over the next decade, worth about $116 billion (Productivity Commission 2025). Yet in the year to June 2026, Australia's GDP per hour worked fell 0.2% (ABS 2026b). AI is everywhere except in the productivity statistics.

This essay argues that the gap is not just a matter of time. It is a bottleneck. AI makes producing work cheap, but it does not make checking that work cheap. Production is a chain of complementary tasks, and when one link speeds up, the slowest remaining link sets the pace (Kremer 1993; Jones & Tonetti 2026). In knowledge work, that slowest link is increasingly human verification.

My forecast is that by 2030, AI will lift the volume of work produced in Australian financial services far faster than it lifts finished, compliant output. Gains at the task level will be large, measured gains in sector productivity modest, and labour will shift toward review rather than disappear, unless verification capacity is deliberately built. Finance is where this constraint bites hardest, because an undetected error there costs more than almost anywhere else.

I use 2030 because it gives AI agents time to spread through banks, insurers and super funds while regulation and review practices are still catching up.

F2What happened in software

Software is where AI agents arrived first, which makes it a preview for other knowledge work. The clearest evidence comes from Chen and Stratton (2026), who tracked about 726,000 workers across 718 firms before and after they adopted AI coding agents.

The agents worked. Lines of code per worker rose 30%, commits 20% and pull requests (packages of code submitted for review) 23%. But finished output did not follow. The number of completed work items barely moved, and the estimates rule out any increase larger than 12%. At most, then, around 0.4 of the coding gain reached the final product. That share is the pass-through.

The missing gains piled up in review. After firms adopted agents, the time from submitting code to approving it rose 49%, from about 7 to 10.5 days. The share of submissions sent back for changes nearly doubled, from 13% to about 25%, and reviewers left 35% more comments on each one. Firms responded by moving people into checking: the share of workers doing code review rose 14%, while overall employment did not change.

AI review tools have not solved the problem. By March 2026 nearly 80% of the firms had used them, yet only 23% of review comments were written by AI (Chen & Stratton 2026). Humans remain the gatekeepers.

Chen and Stratton's model explains why. AI raises the volume of draft work, so there is more to check, and it can lower its quality, so each draft needs more checking. Hiring more reviewers fixes the first problem. Only better technology fixes the second. The result is what many workers would recognise as "AI slop": a growing pile of unchecked output on human desks, and no more finished products leaving the building.

F3Why finance will bottleneck harder

Financial services is the next industry in line. AI use among Australian financial and insurance businesses jumped from 1% in 2021–22 to 24% in 2024–25, the fastest rise of any industry and double the all-business average of 12% (ABS 2026a). Banks are also large software employers in their own right: the Commonwealth Bank ranks first in Asia-Pacific for AI maturity and is training its engineers in AI coding (Australian Broker 2026).

The mechanism runs the same way. AI agents draft credit assessments, statements of advice, compliance reports and customer responses, so there are more drafts per worker. But every draft must be checked against legal duties such as responsible lending, the best-interests duty and anti-money-laundering rules, and the checking is done by senior, accountable staff. Finished, compliant output can grow only as fast as that checking capacity allows.

Here the finance case goes further than the software evidence. In Chen and Stratton's model, the right amount of checking per draft rises with the cost of an undetected error. In software, a missed bug means a fix and a delay. In finance, it can mean regulatory penalties and customer remediation: over eight years of remediation, six major institutions paid or offered $4.7 billion in compensation for fees charged for no service and for non-compliant advice (Australian Broker 2023). Because errors cost so much, review already takes a larger share of a finance firm's costs, and the model shows the shortfall in finished output grows with that share. The bottleneck should therefore be bigger in finance than in software.

The regulator's own evidence points the same way. ASIC reviewed 624 AI use cases across 23 licensees and found that adoption is outrunning governance: of the 14 licensees planning to expand AI, only one had upgraded its governance first (ASIC 2024). Generative AI made up 5% of use cases in production but 22% of those in development, so the larger wave of generated drafts is still to come.

The likely structural result is concentration: firms with deep review capacity, such as the big four banks, can absorb more AI output than smaller lenders.

F4Three scenarios to 2030

The scenarios start from today's sector: 552,900 workers, 3.8% of Australian employment (Jobs and Skills Australia 2025), with output up 4.8% over the year to June 2026 (ABS 2026b). In each, AI use rises from the ABS's 24% of firms toward 72–80% by 2030. AI can draft or assist with about 44% of work hours (Microsoft & Tech Council of Australia 2023) and lifts output on that work by 30% (Chen & Stratton 2026), so by 2030 the volume of drafts rises about 10% in every scenario. What differs is how much of it gets checked.

Baseline: the review backlog. AI agents spread across most large financial firms by about 2028, but senior reviewers remain scarce and slow to retrain. Firms move staff into checking at the same pace the software firms did, but in finance each AI draft needs more checking. Pass-through falls to about 0.12, against 0.20 in software: drafts rise 9.5% by 2030 while finished output rises just 1.1%. Once the cost of errors that slip through is counted, the net gain is roughly zero, and a backlog of unchecked work builds every year. Jobs shift toward review and assurance with little net loss, but graduate hiring softens.

Upside: verification catches up. Firms invest in automated checking and redesign workflows, while regulators set clear expectations for checking AI output. Checking capacity keeps pace and auditable AI takes on routine checks, so almost every extra draft gets through: finished output rises about 10.6%, or about 8% after error costs. Trigger: ASIC and APRA issue AI assurance guidance under their existing powers.

Downside: slop accumulation. Firms let AI rubber-stamp AI to capture the volume gains. Output looks strong at first, about 4% higher in 2026, because unchecked drafts ship. Then errors surface as breaches, followed by remediation and enforcement, and junior roles are cut because AI seems to do their work, shrinking the pool of future senior reviewers. By 2029 net output is about 3.5% below a world without AI. Trigger: a major AI-caused incident brings the government's paused mandatory guardrails back as a blunt fix. The warning signs are already visible: one industry survey found 72% of Australian financial institutions use AI agents in underwriting, but only 3% say their data is fully ready (Australian Broker 2026).

Testing the Industry Week claim. At Industry Week, Dave Lemphers of Maincode argued that AI will change work profoundly, but that the opportunities it creates will outweigh what it displaces (Lemphers 2026). He has elsewhere described AI as "a productivity lift for Australian businesses" (Lemphers 2025). The evidence here supports his view on jobs, since the software firms that adopted agents saw no fall in employment. But it qualifies his view on productivity. A lift in tasks is not a lift in output: on the software evidence, less than half of the gain gets through. Lemphers' optimism holds in full only in the upside scenario, which makes the investment in checking capacity the hinge between his forecast and the baseline.

F5Policy playbook

Australia has chosen not to pass a dedicated AI law. The National AI Plan put the proposed mandatory guardrails "on hold" and left AI risk to existing laws and regulators, supported by a new AI Safety Institute (Department of Industry, Science and Resources 2025). The Productivity Commission agreed that AI-specific regulation should be a last resort, and called instead for gap analyses of existing frameworks (Productivity Commission 2025).

I accept that approach, but in finance the gap is already visible: checking capacity is not keeping pace with AI output. ASIC's finding that only one in 14 expanding licensees upgraded its governance first (ASIC 2024) is the gap analysis the Commission asked for. The policy goal should be to move the sector from the baseline to the upside scenario, using instruments that already exist.

The natural home for the first lever is APRA's operational risk standard, CPS 230, in force since July 2025, which already requires banks, insurers and super funds to define critical operations, manage service providers and report incidents (Brightlaw 2025). It does not mention AI, but AI that supports critical operations would fall within its scope.

Can AI check the AI? Partly. AI checkers add capacity cheaply, but they tend to share the blind spots of the AI that wrote the draft, and a licensee, not a model, carries legal responsibility for advice and lending decisions. Used as an accountable assistant, AI checking moves the sector toward the upside. Used as a rubber stamp, it is what produces the downside.

Extending the Industry Week claim. Lemphers warns that without local capability Australia is "exposed to... opaque black-box systems" (Lemphers 2025). He frames this as a sovereignty problem, but it is also a checking problem. A model a firm cannot inspect is harder to verify, and 30% of the AI use cases ASIC reviewed relied on models built by third parties (ASIC 2024). Transparent, auditable models, whether built locally or required by contract, lower the cost of checking each draft. So auditability should count as part of AI assurance: it is one of the few levers that tackles the quality problem directly.

The EU shows the cost of the opposite approach. Its AI Act classes credit scoring as high-risk, but compliance has already been pushed back from August 2026 to December 2027 (Gibson Dunn 2026). Comprehensive AI laws are slow to implement; targeted guidance through existing regulators can move faster.

F6Methods, data and uncertainty

Method. The forecast uses a simple scenario model calibrated to Australian data. Each year it compares how much checking the AI-boosted volume of drafts needs with how much checking people and AI checkers can do; whatever cannot be checked becomes backlog, so pass-through is calculated rather than assumed. The model reproduces the software results: with full adoption, drafts rise 30%, checking staff 14% and finished output about 6% (Chen & Stratton 2026). It then applies Australian inputs: 2026 employment (Jobs and Skills Australia 2025), AI adoption (ABS 2026a) and the 44% exposure share (Microsoft & Tech Council of Australia 2023). The code, data and every assumption are in the project repository.

Limitations. Four uncertainties matter most.

  • Transferability. The core evidence comes from software firms, mostly outside Australia. Finance tasks differ, so applying the software calibration here is an assumption, not a measurement.
  • Timing. The software estimates cover roughly the first year after adoption. Firms may reorganise around AI over time, as they did with earlier general-purpose technologies (Brynjolfsson, Rock & Syverson 2018), which would push outcomes toward the upside.
  • Structural breaks. A step-change in AI reliability would shrink the quality problem and invalidate the baseline. A major AI-caused incident could trigger a sudden regulatory response.
  • Measurement. Much of what banks and insurers produce is imputed in the national accounts rather than measured directly from sales (Gordon et al. 2016). AI's effects, or their absence, will therefore be hard to see in official statistics. Self-reported gains are no substitute: in a CSIRO trial of an AI assistant, 30% of users reported no productivity benefit (Whittle 2025).
